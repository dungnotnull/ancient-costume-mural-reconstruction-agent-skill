# Period Construction Methods Reference

## Chinese Garment Construction: Historical Techniques

### Han Dynasty (206 BCE - 220 CE)

**Characteristics:**
- Rectangle-based construction (minimal waste cutting)
- Wrapover design (right-over-left lapel required by sumptuary law)
- Narrow sleeves for commoners, wide sleeves for elite
- Deep side openings (for women's garments, facilitating nursing)
- Belt/sash primary fastening

**Construction Sequence:**
1. Cut two rectangular body panels (full fabric width)
2. Attach sleeves (cut continuous with body or separately)
3. Sew side seams (leave deep opening for women's garments)
4. Add neckband (finished edge for neckline)
5. Hem bottom edge
6. Attach belt/sash

**Key Seams:**
- Running stitch for main seams
- Overcast seam edges for finishing
- Backstitch for stress points (armholes, neckline)

**Typical Dimensions:**
- Fabric width: ~50cm (handloom silk)
- Body panel: 2-3 fabric widths
- Sleeve width: 30cm (narrow) to 100cm+ (wide)
- Garment length: Waist to ankle (variable by status)

---

### Tang Dynasty (618 - 907 CE)

**Characteristics:**
- Mix of rectangular and fitted elements
- Round neckline becomes standard (both men and women)
- Sash/belt remains primary fastening
- Collar variations: stand collar, band collar, round collar
- Complex sleeve constructions (narrow cuff, wide upper)
- Layering system: undergarment, main robe, outer jacket

**Construction Sequence:**
1. Cut body panels (may be shaped at shoulder)
2. Attach sleeves (set-in or raglan-style)
3. Construct collar (stand or band)
4. Add fastening (ties, buttons, belt loops)
5. Side seams (may include gussets for shaping)
6. Hem and finish

**Key Seams:**
- Backstitch for structural seams
- Running stitch for assembly
- French seams for fine garments

**Typical Dimensions:**
- Fabric width: ~50-60cm (improved loom technology)
- Sleeve width: Variable, wide upper narrowing to cuff
- Garment length: Floor-length for formal robes

---

### Song Dynasty (960 - 1279 CE)

**Characteristics:**
- More fitted garments emerge
- Set-in sleeve construction becomes common
- Front opening with ties/buttons (new fastening types)
- Refined seam finishing
- Fine gauze fabrics require careful handling

**Construction Sequence:**
1. Cut fitted body panels (shaped pieces)
2. Set in sleeves (armhole construction)
3. Add collar (band or stand)
4. Apply fastening (ties, buttons, frogs)
5. Finish seams (overcast, bound)
6. Hem

**Key Seams:**
- Set-in sleeve seams (new technique)
- Bound seam finishes
- Overcast edges for gauze

**Typical Dimensions:**
- Fabric width: ~60cm
- Closer body fit (reduced ease)
- Sleeve construction more complex

---

### Ming/Qing Dynasties (1368 - 1911 CE)

**Characteristics:**
- Highly codified construction by rank
- Complex fastening systems (frogs, toggles, loops)
- Multi-layered assembly (5+ layers for court dress)
- Stiff formal fabrics (brocades, heavy silks)
- Rank-specific patterns (embroidered badges)

**Construction Sequence:**
1. Assemble multiple underlayers
2. Construct main garment with complex fastening
3. Add rank badges or insignia
4. Attach outer layers
5. Add accessories (sash, belt, headwear)
6. Final fitting and adjustment

**Key Seams:**
- Complex fastening construction
- Embroidery applied before assembly
- Multiple interlining layers

## Non-Chinese Construction Methods

### Central Asian (Sogdian, Uighur)

**Characteristics:**
- More fitted garments (influence from West)
- Introduced new fastening types (buttons, metallic closures)
- Different sleeve constructions
- Often combined with Chinese elements in Silk Road contexts

## General Principles

### Fabric Behavior

**Silk (Mulberry)**
- Lightweight to medium weight (40-100 gsm)
- Good drape, fluid movement
- Requires careful seam finishing (frays easily)
- Degummed silk: soft, lustrous
- Raw silk: stiffer, less lustrous

**Hemp/Ramie**
- Stiff, inelastic drape
- Strong, durable
- Visible fiber ends (hairy surface)
- Requires different seaming (less stretch)

### Grain Orientation

**Warp-grain (vertical)**
- Standard for body panels
- Provides vertical strength
- Minimal stretch

**Weft-grain (horizontal)**
- Crosswise threads
- Some stretch
- Used for sleeves, bindings

**Bias (diagonal)**
- 45-degree angle to grain
- Maximum stretch and drape
- Rarely used in early Chinese garments
- Becomes more common in later periods

### Seam Allowances

**Narrow (2-3mm)**: Fine silk, delicate fabrics
**Medium (5-8mm)**: Standard silk, medium-weight fabrics
**Wide (10mm+)**: Heavy fabrics, wool, winter garments

## Construction Verification

### How to Verify Reconstruction

1. **Check against extant examples**: Does pattern shape match surviving garments?
2. **Check period capability**: Were tools/techniques available?
3. **Check iconographic consistency**: Does silhouette match visual sources?
4. **Check material constraints**: Would fabric behave this way?
5. **Check cultural appropriateness**: Does this match period conventions?
